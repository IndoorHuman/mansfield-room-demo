## What this is

A room on a computer that reads what you have already written, and gives some
of it back to you a few things at a time.

You cannot see that happen to your own writing here, because your writing is
yours and it never leaves your own machine. So this room has somebody else's in
it: **Katherine Mansfield's diary, 1914–1922.** Two hundred and six days. She
died in 1923 and her writing belongs to everyone now.

Press a date on the calendar and you are standing in it. The room holds only
what she had written by then — fifty-odd things in the summer of 1914, nearly
four hundred by the end of 1922. You watch a life accumulate.

## What it claims, and what it costs

- **Your old writing comes back to you, a few things at a time.** The drip is
  the point, not a limitation of it.
- **It is free, and nothing you bring in leaves your machine.**
- **The price is hours.** Not money — time. On one real library it took most of
  an evening before the room was worth opening, and how long it takes depends
  entirely on what you bring. Nothing on screen tells you which run you got.
- **Writing first. Pictures are much slower** and much less certain.
- This is **the first room of a larger house**, and it is still being built.

## Three things wrong with it

1. **Bringing writing in is one lump.** There is no resume. Close the lid, lose
   the power, or restart part-way and the whole import is discarded.
2. **The doors for bringing things in are Mac-only**, and no support is promised.
3. **If it stops answering, it may simply say nothing** rather than say it
   failed — so silence does not always mean "no opinion".

Each of those has a plan behind it. None of them is fixed today.

## Why a dead woman's diary is in the shop window

The room's central promise is that **it will not read your diary until you say
so**. Fenced writing is not merely hidden from you — it never reaches the part
that reads at all.

Demonstrating that needs real, private, interior writing, and the most private
writing is exactly what copyright holds longest. Mansfield is the rare writer
whose whole life, diary included, is in the public domain. So the fence could be
shown over something genuinely raw instead of over placeholders.

**One of her days is still held back here.** The room cannot see it — not the
words, not the date, not that it exists. You may let it through, watch that one
day fill in, and it goes back to being held for the next person.

## Two things you will notice

**It writes *to* her** — *"you set that down"* — because it always writes to
whoever it is reading, and nobody told it she was gone.

**Some of her days come back with nothing.** It will not write about a person it
cannot speak to, and her quietest years are the ones it has least to say about.
Those days are marked. Her own writing is on every one of them.

## Look, don't touch

Everything here was made in advance, on one computer, before this page existed.
**Nothing is thinking while you look at it.** There is no key behind this page
and nothing here can be made to spend money.

What you do lasts as long as your visit. Keep a reflection and it goes on the
shelf; reload and the shelf is bare again. The next visitor gets a fresh room.

## How it is built

- **One static page and a copy of the room's own front end** — the same files
  that ship, not a rebuild.
- **The whole server is replaced by a stand-in of about ninety lines**, because
  the surface turned out to be tiny: everything goes through one read helper and
  one write helper. Reads answer from a snapshot captured off the real server.
  **Writes are kept in memory for the visit and gone on reload** — which is what
  makes look-don't-touch enforced rather than promised.
- The calendar is a real object in the room: a 44×40 sprite drawn in the room's
  own palette, hanging on the wall above the desk.
- Nothing is stored in your browser. No cookies, no service worker, no database.

## Where it came from

Mansfield's diary, letters, poems and short fiction are **public domain
worldwide** — she died in 1923, the *Journal* was published in 1927 and the
*Letters* in 1929.

The reflections were written by the room itself, in advance, one day at a time,
on one machine. They are quoted whole and unedited, including the ones that are
awkward.

## A note on search engines

This page currently asks search engines and archives to stay away, because every
word on it is still a stand-in. That is a deliberate hold and it comes off in
one line, in two files, once the wording is hers.
