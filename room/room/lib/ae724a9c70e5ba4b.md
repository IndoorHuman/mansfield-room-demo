"Having read the whole of \"The Idiot\" through again, and fairly carefully, I feel slightly more bewildered than I did before as regards Nastasya Filipovna's character." The care and the bewilderment arrive together there, one after the other in the same sentence, and neither cancels the other out. Then the verdict, twice, in short breath: "She is really not well done. She is badly done." And after all that reading, still the open question — "What was Dostoevsky really aiming at?"

You were unwilling to let the first impressions go, either. "Dark and deep, passionate and disdainful" stayed copied out, and so did "a balked fascination, which almost succeeds finally in blotting out those first and really marvellous ' impressions ' of her." Almost. You put the word in.

With Shatov it goes differently. "He is just what we thought him; he behaves just as we would expect him to do." And then the thing you say he realises — "through a little squealing baby — the miracle just being alive is." You know how the chapter ends. You wrote it down anyway: "Every time I read those chapters about his newborn happiness I cherish a kind of tiny hope that this time he will escape — he will be warned, he won't die."

A few pages on, someone knocks: "A man has come to clean the windows. I might have known it!" You turn it straight into the last knock there is, and then the room comes back in its own detail — the smoke, the gas bubbling "as if the pipes were full of water", the quiet, the cold you have. "B. has given me his fountain pen." "I feel absolutely alive after my experience of this afternoon."

I keep setting his miracle and your absolutely alive side by side, since the same hand put both down. Did you see them as the same sentence, or did the second one surprise you when it came?
