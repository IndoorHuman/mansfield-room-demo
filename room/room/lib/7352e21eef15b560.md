"I wonder why it should be so very difficult to be humble." That is the first line, and the page never really leaves it. "I do not think I am a good writer; I realize my faults better than anyone else could realize them. I know exactly where I fail." And then, a few lines on, the thing that undoes the modesty: "when I have finished a story and before I have begun another, I catch myself preening my feathers."

The root gets a shape — "a root of it that puts out a thick shoot on the slightest provocation" — and the shoot is treated as a work problem before it is treated as a moral one. "This interferes very much with work." Then two orders given in the flattest voice: "Calm yourself. Clear yourself." And the reason: "anything that I write in this mood will be no good; it will be full of sediment."

What sits with me is how the trying is described as a physical arrangement. "If I were well, I would go off by myself somewhere and sit under a tree." Not a resolution, a place. And forgetting oneself is put down as a skill with a method — "One must learn, one must practise, to forget oneself" — practise being the word, as if it had scales.

The hardest lines are unhedged and I will not soften them: "Oh God! I am divided still. I am bad. I fail in my personal life. I lapse into impatience, temper, vanity, and so I fail as thy priest." Then, immediately after, without transition: "Perhaps poetry will help."

And after all of it, a new paragraph, a whole change of register: "I have just thoroughly cleaned and attended to my fountain pen. If after this it leaks, then it is no gentleman!"

I keep wondering whether the pen and the divided self are the same problem in your hands — something attended to thoroughly, then held to its word — or whether the pen is simply what was on the desk when the writing stopped.
