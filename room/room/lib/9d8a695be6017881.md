"Weather worse than ever. At tea-time I surprised myself by breaking down." The surprise is in the sentence itself — not that it happened, but that it came without notice, between the weather and the tea. "I simply felt for a moment overcome with anguish and came upstairs and put my head on the black cushion." Then, with no bridge at all: "My longing for cities engrosses me."

That last line stands on its own feet. It is not an explanation of the cushion. It is its own subject, and it is the one that gets the strong verb.

The day before, the room downstairs is inventoried like something you meant to keep: "My sewing-basket is on the table: under the bookcase are poked J.'s old house shoes. The black chair, half in shadow, looks as if a happy person had sprawled there." And the dinner — "roast mutton and onion sauce and baked rice" — with the verdict after it, "It sounds right." Then the ribbons: "I have run the ribbons through my underclothes with a hair-pin in the good home way."

So there are two black things in two days, the chair with a happy person's shape still in it and the cushion your head went down on, and you did not connect them anywhere on the page.

What you did write, in the warm room with the wind outside, was "It looks like a real room where real people live" — looks like, as if you were standing at the door of it rather than in the chair. And what does the longing want, exactly, when it engrosses you: the cities themselves, or the being someone who is on the way to one?
