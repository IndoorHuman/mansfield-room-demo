"My feeling about Ernestine is shameful." That is where it begins, and the next line refuses to take it back: "But there it is." Then the list, which you did not round off or apologise for — "Her tread, her look, the way her nose is screwed round, her intense stupidity, her wrists — revolt me. This is bad."

What you do next is not defend it. You turn it toward her: "For she feels it, I am sure she does." And then further: "I feel that her selfrespect is shamed by my thoughts." The judgement stays where you put it, and so does the discomfort of knowing it lands on somebody.

Then the page changes hands entirely. "Lumbago. This is a very queer thing. So sudden, so painful." And immediately after that, without a break in the thinking: "I must remember it when I write about an old man." The pain arrives and is put straight to work — "The start to get up — the pause — the look of fury — and how, lying at night, one seems to get locked."

I keep going back to the one that follows: "To move is an agony; till finally one discovers a movement which is possible." I wonder whether the old man ever did turn up on a later page with that pause of yours already in him.
