"Our house in Tinakori Road stood far back from the road." The house comes first and the road comes second, and between them is all that distance you spend the next sentences going down: "the garden sloped away in terraces and flights of concrete steps — down — until you reached the stone wall covered with nasturtiums that had three gates let into it."

The gates get counted and named. "the visitors' gate, the Tradesman's gate, and a huge pair of old iron gates that were never used and clashed and clamoured when Bogey and I tried to swing on them." The one that was never used is the only one that makes a sound.

Then the ledger of what is over the fence. "Tinakori Road was not fashionable; it was very mixed." Father is quoted on the value of land and on the tramway; Mother is quoted on one word, 'hovel,' and you kept her quotation marks. The old man who burned leather "whenever the wind blew our way." A garden "planted with empty jam tins and old saucepans and black iron kettles without lids" — the lids missing, specifically, which nobody has to say.

And the last thing before the bracketed note is not a house at all but a place under things: "below the paling fence in a hollow, squeezed in almost under the fold of a huge gorse-covered hill, was Saunder's Lane."

The whole piece walks downhill. High and healthy at the top with the sun pouring in all the windows all day long, and then down the terraces, down the steps, past the wall, across the road, under the fence, into the hollow. Does it stop there because the writing ran out, or because that was where you were going the whole time?
