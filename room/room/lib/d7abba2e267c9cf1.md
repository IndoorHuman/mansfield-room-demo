"Played cribbage with J. I delight in seeing him win." You put the delight first, before you say why, and the why comes in the next breath: "When we play he sometimes makes faces at me — the same kind of faces that Chummie used to make." And then, plainly, "I think I am never so fond of him as when he does this."

There is no softening around that sentence. The face crosses from one person to another and you let it, and the fondness arrives with it.

Then the cat, who takes up more of the page than the card game does. "It is true he has become as real as if he could talk. I feel he does talk, and that when he is silent it is only a case of making his nettle shirt and he will begin." You give him an instrument — "playing his fiddle with wool for strings" — and a piano, and Nelly Bly, and a love named Isbel, and "his whole complete little life side by side with ours."

And then: "I shall never tell it, though."

That "though" is doing a great deal. It comes after you have already told a good deal of it — the fiddle, the shirt, Isbel — so what is left, the part that would be the real telling? I wonder whether the thing you meant never to tell is the same thing as the faces across the cribbage board, or something else altogether.

"We were talking of the personality of the cat to-day and saying that we ought to write it down."
