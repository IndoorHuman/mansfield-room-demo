"Important." That is how the page opens — one word standing on its own before the sentence it introduces, "When we can begin to take our failures non-seriously, it means we are ceasing to be afraid of them. It is of immense importance to learn to laugh at ourselves."

Twice in two lines: important, immense importance. You do not often underline yourself like that.

Then the page turns and does not stay laughing. "What will happen to Anatole France and his charming smile? Doesn't it disguise a lack of feeling, like M.'s weariness?" A smile put on the stand and questioned, immediately after arguing for derision's value.

"Life should be like a steady, visible light."

And under it, plainly: "What remains of all those years together? It is difficult to say. If they were so important, how could they have come to nothing. Who gave up and why?" No answer follows. The question mark is missing from the middle sentence, as though it had already stopped being a question by the time you wrote it.

Then the old argument, still the same one: "Haven't I been saying, all along, that the fault lies in trying to cure the body and paying no heed whatever to the sick psyche? G. claims to do just what I have always dreamed might be done."

And last, with nothing attached to it, nothing asked of it: "The sound of a street pipe, hundreds and hundreds of years old." I keep wondering whether that one came in through the window while you were writing the rest, or whether you went looking for it to close on.
