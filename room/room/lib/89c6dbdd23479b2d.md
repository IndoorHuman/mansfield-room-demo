"Calm day. In garden read early poems in Oxford Book. Discussed our future library. In the evening read Dostoevsky." Four sentences, and then a fifth that goes back on itself: "In the morning discussed the importance of eternal life."

The evening is written down before the morning. Nothing marks the turn, no correction, no crossing out — the day simply gets recounted twice over, once forward and once from a different angle, as though the eternal life had been remembered while the pen was already moving on. I wonder whether the order of a day ever really settles, or whether it keeps rearranging itself for as long as anyone is still writing it.

And then, with the same evenness given to Dostoevsky and to eternity: "Played our famous Stone Game (Cape Sixpence and Cornwall)."

The note underneath explains it fully and with great patience. "You placed a largish stone at the extreme edge of a cliff, sat down about ten yards away and shied smaller stones at it. The one who first toppled it over received sixpence from the other." A cliff got named for the sixpence. "Discussed our future library" sits three lines above a game whose whole rule is throwing small stones at a large one until it goes over the edge, and both belong to the calm day.

"Hence the name, Cape Sixpence, which we gave to the cliff near Bandol where we first played the game."
