"Broke through. This is a great relief." Three words and then four, and nothing around them to say what broke or what it broke out of. It sits between the dream and the birds like a door swinging once.

Before it, the dream: "A long typical boat dream. I was, as usual, going to N.Z. But for the first time my stepmother was very friendly — so nice. I loved her." And then, without a change of voice, "She disappeared, and it was too late to find her or tell her to come back at last." You let the kindness and the too-late stand in the same paragraph, and you did not go back to soften either one.

After it, the sill. "When I went to the window all the food was gone, but there was the tiny print of their feet on the sill." You did not see them come. You saw that they had come. "Very soon, terrified, however, one came, then another, then a third, balanced on the cocoanut. They are precious little atoms."

The snow gets the flattest sentence of the day: "I think I hate snow, downright hate it. There is something stupefying in it, a kind of ' You must be worse before you're better,' and down it spins." And then straight out of that, without pause: "I love, I long for the fertile earth."

"J. and I read Mansfield Park with great enjoyment." That one has no hedge in it at all, which is what makes the line after it stand out — "I wonder if J. is as content as he appears? It seems too good to be true." You put the question to the page rather than to him. I keep wondering whether the wondering itself was a pleasure to you, some days, the way a thing turned over in the hand is.
