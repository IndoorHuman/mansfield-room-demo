"I have broken the silence. It took long."

That is the first line, and everything after it is addressed to someone. "Did I fail you when I sat reading? Oh, bear with me a little. I will be better. I will do all, all that we would wish. Love, I will not fail."

Then the weather comes in and takes over the page: "To-night it is very wild. Do you hear? It is all wind and sea." The question is asked of him, not of us. And the world in it is not solid — it is "blowing like a feather, springing and rocking in the air like a balloon from Lindsay's."

In the middle of the wind there is a piano, and it is corrected on the spot: "I seem to hear a piano sometimes, but that's fancy." I keep stopping there. The hearing and the taking-back are in the same breath, and she lets both stand.

The plan she sets herself is small and daily: "If I write every day faithfully a little record of how I have kept faith with you — that is what I must do." What follows is not a record but a person walking in — "You are stepping forward, one hand in your pocket. My brother, my little boy brother! Your thoughtful eyes!" — seen exactly as he was last seen, "I see you always as you left me."

And the thing she cannot get past is not the leaving but a moment before it: "I saw you a moment alone — by yourself — and quite lost, I felt." I wonder whether that is what the whole entry is for — not the faithful little record at all, but getting back to that one minute and standing next to him in it.

"Now I will come quite close to you, take your hand, and we shall tell this story to each other."
