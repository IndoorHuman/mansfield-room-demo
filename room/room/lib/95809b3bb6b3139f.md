The desk moves first, and the reason comes after it: "Perhaps I shall be able to write far more easily here." Then the second thought, arriving as if checked against the room — "Yes, this is a good place for the desk, because I cannot see out of the stupid window. I am quite private."

What interests me is what happens once the window is out of the way. The page does not stay in the corner. The lamp's rays fall on "the yellow and green Indian curtain and on the strip of red embroidery," and then, with the outside deliberately blocked, you go straight out into it anyway: "I love to close my eyes a moment and think of the land outside, white under the mingled snow and moonlight — the heaps of stones by the roadside white — snow in the furrows."

The other page runs the same way and faster. A letter arrives, and "His personality comes right through his letters to J. and I want to laugh and run into the road."

So the road appears twice — once seen with the eyes shut, once as somewhere to run. I keep wondering whether the corner was ever really about being shut in, or whether it is the place from which the far white fields are easiest to reach.

"The forlorn wind scarcely breathes."
