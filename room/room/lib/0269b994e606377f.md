"Travelling is terrible. All is so sordid, and the train shatters one. Tunnels are hell. I am frightened of travelling."

The fear comes last, after the reasons, as though it had to be argued into before it could be said plainly. Four short sentences and none of them lean on each other.

Then the sentence turns on a but: "We arrived in Paris late, but it was very beautiful — all emerging from water." And after that, "In the night I looked out and saw the men with lanterns." Nothing is asked of the men with lanterns. They are simply what was out there when you looked.

The word comes back — "The hotel all sordid again, — fruit peelings, wastepaper, boots, grime, illtemper." A list, and the last item on it is not an object.

About the appointment, you wrote it twice over: "But on the way there, nay, even before, I realized my heart was not in it." You corrected yourself mid-sentence to make the knowing earlier than it was. And then: "He said through her he could cure me completely. But I did not believe it. It all seemed suddenly unimportant and ugly."

What stays with me is the instruction you set yourself in the middle of it: "Don't forget, as I rang the bell, the scampering and laughter inside." Standing outside a wrong door, hearing other people's laughter through it, and telling yourself to keep it. I wonder what that one was being kept for — whether it was already going somewhere, or whether it was enough that it happened and someone had heard it.

And the page ends where it ends: "But the flat was nice — the red curtains, marble clock, and picture of ladies with powdered hair."
