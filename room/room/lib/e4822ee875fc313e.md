"H. came. He says my right lung is practically all right. Can one believe such words?"

The question comes straight after the good news, in the same breath, and then another one comes: "Can this be true?" And then a third: "Is this fantastic?" Three questions to hold one hopeful afternoon, and none of them answered on the page. What you did put down plainly, with no question in it at all, was his hopefulness — "He was so hopeful to-day that T.B. seemed no longer a scourge. It seemed that one recovered more often than not."

Then the papers. "Tore up and ruthlessly destroyed much. This is always a great satisfaction." You give the reason immediately: "Whenever I prepare for a journey I prepare as though for death. Should I never return, all is in order. This is what life has taught me." It is set down as a practice, not a fear — the tidying and the satisfaction arrive together in the same sentence.

And the day ends with two people and a card game. "J. and I seem to have played cribbage off and on all day. I feel there is much love between us. Tender love." The word "seem" is doing something there — as if the day was not planned around the board, only kept returning to it between other things. A letter to O. that took a week sits in the same evening as a game that took no deciding at all.

I keep wondering which of those two was the easier to begin.

"Let it not change."
