"The heavens opened for the sunset to-night." That is the whole first line, and everything after it is you trying to say what it did — "came a burst of heavenly bright petals....", and then sitting "behind the window, pricked with rain", and then the thing that surprises me every time: "that hard thing in my breast melted and broke into the smallest fountain, murmuring as aforetime, and I drank the sky and the whisper."

And out of a sunset comes an argument about method. "Now who is to decide between 'Let it be' and 'Force it'?" J. gets the whip and the idle steed that "shies at such a journey in prospect." You get the other side of it, and you put it as a picture rather than a principle: "if mine does not gallop and dance at free will, I am not riding at all, but just swinging from its tail." It is not a settled position. It is a question you left standing, with a shrug of evidence after it — "For example, to-day.... To-night he's all sparks."

The May page is the same quarrel with the weather taken out of it. "To-day is Sunday. It is raining a little, and the birds are cheeping. There's a smell of food and a noise of chopping cabbage." Cabbage, birds, rain — and then, without a paragraph of warning, "Oh, if only I could make a celebration and do a bit of writing. I long and long to write, and the words just won't come. It's a queer business." You don't stay in the not-coming for long. The next breath is "when I read people like Gorky, for instance, I realise how streets ahead of them I be...."

So the two of them stand together: a night the sky opened and the hard thing melted, and a Sunday where the longing was loud and the words were not. Both are written by someone who trusts the horse. What I keep wondering is whether "all sparks" ever announces itself beforehand, or whether it only ever gets named afterwards, in the writing down.
