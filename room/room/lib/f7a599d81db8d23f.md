"Does one ever know? One never knows." It opens with the question already answered, and then goes on asking anyway — "how foolish it would be to ask the question: 'What are you thinking of?'" — and further down, the doubt does not close: "Even if she asked, how could she be certain he did not make up the answer."

Then the bracket, [An unposted letter.], and the page changes register entirely. No more one and she. Now: "Your letters sounded insincere to me; I did not believe them. People don't write such things; they only think they do, or they read them in books. But real life is on quite another plane."

The two halves say opposite things about what can be known. The first says certainty is not available at all. The second is willing to name insincerity outright and refuse it — "I could have answered your letter just in your vein and accepted ' it, you knowing how I accepted it and I knowing that you knew — but it wouldn't have lasted."

And the middle of it, the part that does not argue with anyone: "life and work are two things indivisible. It's only by being true to life that I can be true to art." That sentence has no addressee. It would stand whether the letter went out or not.

The word she stops on is adventure — "Friendship is an adventure; but do we agree about the meaning of the word ' adventure '?" She marks it as the place the quarrel would happen, which is a strange and generous thing to notice about a word. I keep wondering which meaning was hers, whether adventure here means the going out or the risk of being known, since the page ends on "Let us be open with each other and not concealing anything" — and was never posted.

"I still wish it were possible; but I cannot, and I won't pretend."
