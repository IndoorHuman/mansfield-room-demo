"Another radiant day." That is where the page starts, and then almost at once it is three false starts and a month to recover, and a story you are no longer sure of — "It seems to me it's a little ' wispy ' — not what it might have been." The G.P. is better, you write, "But that is not good enough, either...." The judgement arrives before the sentence has even finished settling.

And then, without a bridge, the blue. "The last few days what one notices more than anything is the blue." "Blue sky, blue mountains." "And clouds of all kinds — wings, soft white clouds, almost hard little golden islands, great mock-mountains. The gold deepens on the slopes." You put a hedge in front of the verdict, as though it needed defending: "In fact, in sober fact, it is perfection."

The part I keep returning to is that the beauty does not console. It measures. "Then with that unearthly beauty before one it is not hard to" — and the page breaks, and comes back with "realise how far one has to go." What you want from it is not rest but a standard: "To write something that will be worthy of that rising moon, that pale light. To be simple ' enough, as one would be simple before God...."

Two things stand close here and do not argue: what the sea and Beryl fanning her hair at the window would not do, and what the evening light asks. "These things would not die down." I wonder whether the late evening earns its place as the time — of times because it is the one hour when the not-good-enough and the perfection are looking at the same thing.
