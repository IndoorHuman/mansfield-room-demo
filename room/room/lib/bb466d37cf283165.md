"The baby became covered with inkspots and served as a little reminder for days of the things she had forgotten to say and the things she might have said so differently."

That sits at the top, and nothing underneath it comes back to explain it.

Then the cat, set inside its own quotation marks — "It sat very still and its little peaked shadow lay beside it...." — and then, outside them, in the plain voice: "I shan't say it like that. It's only a note." So the thing gets put down twice, once as writing and once as not-yet-writing, and then straight after: "I shall not forget you, my little cat, as you ran along your beat on this whirling earth."

Twice the page stops and puts up the same two words on their own line, "Why Suffer?", once over the woolen shirt and once over the two people who "looked at each other and for some reason they smiled, actually smiled." Under the second one the music comes in and says "All will be well" and "Life is so easy... so easy," and then the elephants drink out of bottles and the clown takes the bottles away and drinks himself.

The waiting-room is written as a thing that ought to be written: the glass doors, the cyclamen like wax, a cart shaking by. And then the frank answer, and the carriage, and the champagne where "the little bubbles hurled themselves to the brim, danced, broke."

And then it is not a story any more. "I should like this to be accepted as my confession." "There is no limit to human suffering." "One pang is eternal torment." I am not going to put anything alongside that to soften the shape of it. The page itself goes on: "One must submit. Do not resist. Take it. Be overwhelmed. Accept it fully. Make it part of life." And, in case anyone reading might think it was said for comfort, "These are not idle words. These are not the consolations of the sick." And then, a few lines later, with the leaves moving in the garden, "It is hard — it is hard to make a good death...."

What is on the same pages, after that, is the harbour: "At last the milk white harbour catches the glitter and the gulls floating on the trembling water gleam like the shadows within a pearl." The dog dragging the heavy chain and kalop-kalops at the water standing cold in the iron pan. The cat coming from nowhere onto the kitchen window sill for her spill of warm morning milk. The children who cannot get up because they "simply must have one more little sleep — the best sleep of all — the warm, soft, darling little rabbit of a sleep.... Just let me hug it one minute more before it bounds away."

I have wondered which came first in the afternoon — the milk white harbour or the confession — and whether the hand that wrote the one had to set anything down before it could write the other.

"To live — to live — that is all."
