"I find the rapture at being alone hard to understand." That is how the page opens, and then it goes on to understand it very well indeed: "Certainly when I am sitting out of sight under a tree I feel I could be content never to return. As to fear,' it is gone."

The question comes next and answers itself in one word. "Should I be as happy with anyone at my side? No." No softening after it, only the reason arriving late — "I'd begin to talk, and it's far nicer not to talk" — and then the little blue book by Diderot, and "I do not want to be a book worm."

After that the watching takes over, and it is the woman at the chalet who gets the longest look: the cradle of two straw baskets, the tiny sleeve pulled up, the daughter danced away from the baby's hand. "She believed and she did not believe."

In the middle of the servant maid you stop to instruct yourself, in brackets, where no one was meant to be reading: "(This must be written very directly.)"

Then the hollow behind the bushes, and what you took from it — "there was nothing to distinguish me, at that moment, from an ordinary human being." It sits between the baby's feet like little cakes of dough and the bird, and nothing introduces it.

The bird is the one thing you leave open. "Each little movement of this bird is made so ostentatiously— as if it were trying to show itself off as much as possible. Why?" Every other why on this page gets an answer within a line or two. I keep wondering whether that Why is really for the bird, or whether the man in the pale blue woollen slippers and the friend with his cap on one side had already put the question in your hand.

And then the afternoon comes down: "Now it's getting late afternoon and all sounds are softer, deeper. The sough of the wind in the branches is more thoughtful." "To look up through the trees to the far-away heavenly blue." "This — this is as great happiness as I shall ever know. It is greater happiness than I had ever thought possible."
