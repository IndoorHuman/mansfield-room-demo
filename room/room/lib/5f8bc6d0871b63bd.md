"We put the chess-men on the board and began to play. It was an unsettled day." The board comes first and the unsettledness after, in the same breath, as though the two were one weather.

Then the question you ask and leave standing: "I had read one of the stories myself and it seemed to me nothing. But read aloud, it was a masterpiece. How was that?" You do not answer it. It sits there, a real question about what a voice does to a page.

And then the evening before, which you say plainly you want to remember: "I saw a face which reminded me of his mother and Richard. But I felt a kind of immediate dread confusion. I knew I ought to know it and that it belonged to him, yet he was as it were not present."

What you do with it is turn outward — to people going out of their minds, to "old, old people about the children," and to "the foolish offended look in their faces sometimes. They feel it's not right they should not know." You took a bad half-minute of your own and gave it away to strangers as an explanation for them.

I keep thinking about the first sentence, though. When a day is going to be unsettled, does the board come out because you already know, or does the knowing arrive later, in the writing of it down?
