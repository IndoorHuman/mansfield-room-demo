"But the point is — Where shall I begin? One certainly wants to dash."

Everything else was already in hand. The Sheridans instead of the Burnells, "the three girls and the brother and the Father and Mother and so on," the sisters Bead who come to stay, "The white sheet on the floor when the wedding dress is tried on." "Yes, I've got the details all right." The details were never what was in the way.

What you kept turning was the order of things. "Meg was playing. I don't think I ought to begin with that. It seems to me the mother's coming home ought to be the first chapter. The other can come later." And then, inside the chapter you had just demoted, the thing you actually wanted: "Which is the real life — that or this? — late afternoon, these thoughts — the garden — the beauty — how all things pass — and how the end seems to come so soon."

The page goes off after a bird at that point. "And then again there is the darling bird — I've always loved birds — Where is the little chap?..." Then: "What is it that stirs one so? What is this seeking — so joyful — ah, so gentle! And there seems to be a moment when all is to be discovered. Yes, that's the feeling...." Then the piano, and "The garden of the Casino, the blue pansies." Then, flat against all of it, "But oh, how am I going to write this story?"

And then it begins anyway, without settling the question: "The late afternoon was beautiful in Port Willin." Late afternoon, the same hour you had been circling three lines earlier. The harbour gold, the hospital windows fiery, and then the exception held back to the end of the paragraph. I find myself wondering whether the silver was there first and the gold arranged around it, or whether the gold had to be laid down before you could see what refused to take it.

"Only the pigeons flying so high above Post Office Square and the plumes of smoke rising from the evening fires were silver."
