"I wish I had not got a cold, / The wind is big and wild, / I wish that I was very old, / Not just a little child." That is where this pool begins, and the child in it does not get better by the end. "It really feels quite like a day / Since I have had my tea; / P'raps everybody's gone away / And just forgotten me." Nobody comes. The wind is still out there "waiting to be fed."

What sits beside it is a whole shelf of rooms at night with a child in them, awake. In "The Candle": "But the awful thing was the window: / I could not think what was outside." And then, having crept out of bed to look — "There was nothing at all to be seen. / But hundreds of friendly candles all over the sky / In remembrance of frightened children." In "Evening Song of the Thoughtful Child" the shadows on the wall get spoken to directly, and kissed goodnight: "And I kiss you--on the wall / On the curtains--on the blind." In "Stars" the same sky, counted from the gate: "There were more stars--more / Than I could have expected, / Even I!"

So the dark is not one thing here. It swallows, and it is also full of small lit objects that were put there on purpose.

Alongside the frightened rooms are the plain delights, kept in the same handwriting. "In the middle of our porridge plates / There was a blue butterfly painted / And each morning we tried who should reach the butterfly first." "That made us laugh. / Always she said it and always it started us laughing." And the sugar planted in the garden — "Because you love it so frightfully / I thought there would be a whole sugar tree for your birthday, / And now it will all be melted."

The likes list holds the same register, only grown: "I love the satiny colour of mushrooms, and their smell and the soft stalks." "I love Christmas; I shall always feel it is a holy time." "I love gentleness." "How I love to think of us as workers, writers — two creatures given over to Art." And then, right there among them, without softening: "I adore Life, but my experience of the world is that it's pretty terrible."

That sentence is doing what the child's poems do — holding both, and not resolving them. The dislikes page keeps the same nerve: "I can't help seeing all the evil and pain in the world: it must be faced and recognised, and I can't bear your sentimentalist or silly optimist." And, in the same breath as all that gravity: "I hate haricot beans."

The reading notes have their own version of a candle by the bed. "On my bed at night there is a copy of Shakespeare, a copy of Chaucer, an automatic pistol and a black muslin fan." "I have been reading Shakespeare as usual." "I have started a new Shakespeare note book." The looking-up of every word — "finding what are inkles and caddises" — is the same instinct as peeping through the slit of the blind. It was better to know.

And on Tchehov: "I hate to think of dear Tchehov in Nice, with toothache, where he says, 'I was in such pain I crawled up the wall.' That just describes it." Then, elsewhere in the same handwriting, an argument for the defence: "As for Tchekhov being damned — why should he be?"

What I keep turning over is how many of these rooms have a doorway in them. The candle fairy comes because "at night, it always means / That Mother's very near." The little boy in his boat wakes and "They were in the rocking chair, / Mother's arms around him--tight." The pillar box "is quite a friend / When Father goes away." Is the friendly thing always something small and lit that stands in for the person who isn't in the room — or does it sometimes get to be the thing itself?

"I want in fact the feeling that all this beauty is in the deepest sense attached to life."

And from the craft page, the line I would keep if I could only keep one: "How marvellous life is, if only one gives oneself up to it! It seems to me that the secret of life is to accept life. Question it as much as you like after, but first accept it."
