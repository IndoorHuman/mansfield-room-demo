"Went to the Albert Hall with B.C. A bad, dull concert." And then, without a turn or an apology for the turn, "But I thought all the while that I'd rather be with musical people than any others."

The page does not stay in the hall. Something breaks the line — "New Zealand M emorie s" sitting on its own, with the letters come loose — and then the sentence lands somewhere else entirely: "they're mine, really."

The cause given is small and sideways. "A violinist (miles away) bent his head and his hair grew like G.'s: that made me think so, I suppose." Miles away, and in brackets, and still enough.

What stays with me is that the entry ends on a claim rather than a scene: "I ought to be able to write about them wonderfully." Not that you will, not when — only that the ability ought to be there, the way a thing owned ought to be at hand. Does the ownership come first, do you think, and the writing after, or is it the other way about?
