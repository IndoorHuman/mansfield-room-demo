"Put my clothes in order. The crocuses in Battersea reminded me of autumn in Bavaria." One line of housekeeping and then the ground opens under it — Battersea in spring pulling up autumn in Bavaria, two seasons and two countries in the same glance. You do not stop to explain the jump. You go straight on to the wet ground, "the grass long and green among the trampled flowers," and then the birds.

The birds are the strangest turn on that page. "Birds are far more savage-looking than the wildest beasts. Thinking of a forest of wild birds — or if the birds ' turned ' even here." You put the word turned in its own little fence of quotation marks, as though you were not quite willing to own it. And then, immediately: "I want to get alone." Then: "The magnolia conspicua is in bud." No transition between the savage forest and the bud. The naming of the magnolia in full, by its proper name, sits at the end like something you were glad to be certain of.

A few days on there is another fine morning, and it does not help. "A splendid fine morning, but as I know I have to go out and change the cheque and pay the bills, I can do nothing and I feel wretched. Life is a hateful business, there's no denying it." You are plain about it and you do not soften it, and I am not going to soften it either. What you set against the parties and the pleasant society is not nothing, though: "I had so much rather lean idly over the bridge and watch the boats and the free, unfamiliar people and feel the wind blow."

That is the same posture as the crocuses and the magnolia — looking out, naming what is there, wanting no one to require anything of you while you do it. The bridge and the wet grass are the same want. I keep wondering whether getting alone, for you, is the condition of being able to see the buds at all, or whether it is the other way round and it is the seeing that makes the aloneness worth having.

"The magnolia conspicua is in bud."
