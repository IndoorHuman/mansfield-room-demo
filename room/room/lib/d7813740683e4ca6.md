The first verdict is hard and quick — "It seemed so infernally petty, and more than that... it was the saying of a vulgar little mind." Then the page turns and takes it nearly all back, in five words, with no argument made for the reversal. You did not cross out the first thing. You let both stand, one under the other.

And the cold comes back as the same word: "The trouble is I am so infernally cold." Infernally petty, infernally cold. The one adverb doing service for a person you disliked at a distance and for your own body at the desk.

The rest is plain about what it was. "I've had an attack of what the doctor calls acute enteritis. I think it was poisoning." "Very high fever and sickness and dysentery and so on. Horrible." The plan changes inside a sentence — "I decided yesterday to go to the Palace, but to-day makes me feel I'll try and see it out here."

What I keep returning to is that the story does not stop for any of it. "I don't know how I may write this next story. It's so difficult. But I suppose I shall." And then, in the brackets, the manuscript dated the fourteenth.

Even laid up on warm milk, the household still gets its portrait: "Burns everything! Leaves us without eggs, and went off for her afternoon yesterday without a word. We didn't even know she was gone." That last line has a kind of relish in it. Was the noticing ever really off duty, or does it simply run on whatever's left when everything else has gone quiet?
