"Wrote at my story, read Shakespeare." That is the first line, and then "Read Goethe, thought, prayed," and then the day arrives cold and fine — and then: "But I felt ill and could do nothing but lie still all day."

The lying still does not stop the page. What comes after it is the raft. "Something has been built, a raft, frail and not very seaworthy; but it will serve." You put the frailty in before the serving, and you kept both. "Before, I was cast into the water when I was ' alone ' — I mean during my illness — and now something supports me."

The Paris line is doing something odd and I keep returning to it: "This going to Paris has been so much more important than it seemed. Now I begin to see it as the result, the ending of all that reading." Not the beginning of anything. The ending of all that reading — as though the books had been going somewhere the whole time and only now arrive.

Then, without a break, without a flourish: "Heard from Pinker that Cassells have taken " A Cup of Tea."" A story taken, set down in the same plain hand as the cold fine day.

And the last line goes somewhere else entirely — French women, and "their impudent confidence in the power of sex." I wonder whether that came from the window or from the reading, or whether it simply walked in the way thoughts do on a day spent lying still.
