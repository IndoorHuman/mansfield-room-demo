"Went to the clinique. I felt that all was wrong there." The next three sentences each take a person and put them down quickly — M. "distrait and a little angry," D. who "as usual sailed over everything" — and then the line that closes them off: "But that means nothing." What the trouble was, or whether it was trouble at all, stays where you left it: "It seemed to me there had been some trouble or some trouble was brewing."

And then the second paragraph, which gets the detail. "The servant there is a very beautiful plump woman with a ravishing smile. Her eyes are grey." The fringe is small, the shawl is little and grey, and there is an apron, and "a pair of rather high boots." The sentence walks: "stepping lightly, with one small plump hand holding the shawl, she opens the door." That is the last thing before the page turns into a list.

The list is columns of names. "The Major and the Lady." "An Unhappy Man." "Lucien." "Down the Sounds." "Aunt Fan." "Honesty." "Best Girl." Beside them the ones with dates already fixed to them — "A Cup of Tea [January 11, 1922]," "Taking the Veil [January 24, 1922]," "The Doll's House [October 24-30, 1921]."

I keep wondering about "Confidences" and "The Dreamers" — whether they had faces yet when you wrote the titles down, or whether a title was sometimes just a place kept open.
