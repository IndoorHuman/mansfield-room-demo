"Stairs were rays of light up which she floated." That is the housemaid gone and the cook risen in her place, and the sentence goes up with her. Then the puddings, "suggested… as though they were compresses: whiting, because they were so ' delicate and harmless.'" You wrote the elevation and the whole apparatus of it before you wrote a word against her.

Then: "The cook is evil." No bridge. The same page, the same woman, the trembling on the sommier, and the scene you had waited for going quietly wrong — "she said how sorry she was and agreed and apologised and quite understood." "So the serpent still slept between us." "Oh! why won't she turn and speak her mind."

What I keep looking at is that you wrote her at the door twice. "She stayed at the door, plucking at a d'oyley." And further down, with the bracket in between, she is sitting in the middle of the room, "hunched, still," and gets up "obedient, like a prisoner when you enter a cell." The prisoner's line is in both. The first time it comes as your belief about her eyes; the second time you have taken out the trembling and the d'oyley and left the posture and the sentence. I do wonder which of those two you set down first, and whether the second felt like a correction or like a different thing altogether.

And then the page stops being yours. "Her first husband was a pawnbroker" — and from there on it is all her I, running without your interruption: the stock found false, the clot of blood, little Bert and the New Forest, the letters written and never sent, the clergyman who said "perhaps God has kept this trial for you until now." You did not go back in to comment. You did not soften the ending, and it does not end — it breaks off mid-word at the crucifix.

The woman you called a fool is the woman whose whole life you took down without cutting. "Well, I felt my religion was going from me."
