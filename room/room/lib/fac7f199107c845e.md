"The feeling that someone was coming towards me is too strong for me to work." You did not stop at the feeling. You gave it a place to happen in: "It was like sitting on a bench at the end of a long avenue in a park and seeing someone far in the distance coming your way." And then, without a break, the sentence turns and it is not you any more but she — "She tries to read. The book is in her hand, but it's all nonsense, and might as well be upside down. She reads the advertisements as though they were part of the articles."

That slip from I to she is the thing I keep returning to. The bed, the papers, the chill — all of that stays plain and first-person. The moment the waiting becomes unbearable, it goes into the third person and gets a park and an avenue built around it.

The page keeps that doubling up. "I must not forget the long talk L.M. and I had the other evening about hate." You set the questions down in a row — "What is hate? Who has ever described it? Why do I feel it for her?" — and then you write out both sides of the talk as dialogue, hers and yours, with the stage direction included: "I reply:' If you were cherished and loved for a week, you would recover.'" You do not soften what follows. "And that is true, and I would like to do it. It seems I ought to do it. But I don't." And then, immediately: "The marvel is that she understands. No one else on earth could understand."

The same doubleness in the little corner. "I may come into my little corner to-night?" she asks timidly, and you record your own voice against yourself — "so cold, so cynical" — before the line that undoes it: "But what would I do if she didn't come?"

Then J. arrives and time goes strange: "In half an hour it seemed he had been here a long time." Then the walk out, and the two sentences that are pure looking — "Bare leafless trees. A wonderful glow in the sky: the windows flashed fire."

And then the drawing. "M drew a picture of my heart. I wish he had not." You are exact about what the harm was: it is not the news, it is the image. "I am haunted by the hideous picture, by the thought of my heart like a heavy drop in my breast." A picture of a thing you had been carrying without seeing, handed to you to see. And still the page will not close on it — "But he is good."

I notice how often you end a hard passage on the small fair thing. Not to cancel it. It sits after it, unexplained, the way the fire in the windows sits after the bare trees. Do you know, when you set them down, which one you mean to be last?
