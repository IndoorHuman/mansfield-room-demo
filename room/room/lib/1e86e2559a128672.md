"Just a line to say — J. and I both have so much work to do this summer that we have decided when we leave here (end of this month) to go to the Hotel d'Angleterre, Randogne." Everything in that sentence is arrangement: the work, the leaving, the month, the hotel with its name in full. And then straight after, before anyone can answer, "Does that make you open your eyes?"

The page keeps moving that way. "It would only take a day to settle and a look at the mountains, before we could work." The mountains are given one clause and are already behind her, because what comes next is the winter at Bandol, and the maid — "I am going to get a maid now at once. I can't do without one." — and the reason, which is not rest but time: "I simply have not the time to attend to everything and I can't bear, as you know, ' untidiness.'" The apostrophes around untidiness are hers. She fences the word off, the way you would hold something at arm's length while still naming it.

Then: "Don't speak of our plans, by chance, will you?"

And then the page stops planning altogether. "There is a really superb professional pianist here. He plays nearly all day and writes his own music." Two sentences, on their own lines, attached to nothing before or after. Nothing is decided about the pianist, nothing arranged. He is the only thing on the page that is simply true right now rather than scheduled. "Au revoir. K.M."

After the signature the page keeps going in someone else's hand — the bracket, the return to Switzerland, the doctor who "was astonished at her progress," and then the harder sentences: that she "never believed that she would die of consumption, but always of heart-failure," that she resolved "to live as though her grave physical illness were incidental, and even, so far as she could, as though it were non-existent." That is written by a man looking back, with "to my great regret" in the middle of it, and it knows how the summer went. Her own part of the page does not, and does not pretend to. Hers ends on a musician working in the next room.

I keep turning over that the pianist gets no explanation. She explains the hotel, the maid, the winter, even the secrecy — and then the one thing she has actually heard with her own ears is handed over plain, twice, in two short lines. I wonder whether that is what she trusted most: the thing she did not have to make any arrangements for.
