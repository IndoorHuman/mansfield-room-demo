"England is like a dream. I am sitting at the window of a little square room furnished with a bed, a wax apple, and an immense flowery clock."

You wrote that room twice. Once as the page begins, and again further down: "a most extraordinary room furnished with a bed, a wax apple and an immense flowery clock." Both times the wax apple, both times the clock, both times the garden "full of wallflowers and blue enamel saucepans." It is the same three objects set down in the same order, as though the room could only be reported and not improved on.

The fear runs right beside it and does not spoil it. "It won't do, it won't do at all," said the colonel, and before that you had already written "I shut my teeth. I kept my fingers from trembling as I handed the passport and the ticket." And then, one line later, "I nearly knelt on the floor." What follows is the muddy street, the flapping cab doors, the bony horse, and "It was like an elopement."

What I keep going back to is the Buffet at Chateaudun — the pale green room, the coloured bottles, the little boy who "served the soldiers with a kind of dreary contempt," and then the old man with his pail of brown spotted fish, standing "humbly waiting for someone to attend to him, his cap in his hands." You gave him the longest sentence in that whole passage. Everyone else in the room is moving and shouting; he is the one who gets the reason written out.

Earlier in the same month the page is short and holds no country at all: "I can do nothing. Have tidied my desk and taken some quinine and that's all." And then: "But I know I shall go, because otherwise I'll die of despair." The desk was tidied and the going was already decided in the same breath. I am not going to make those two Februaries agree with each other. They are both there.

By May the walking has become something you play at. "I crossed and recrossed the river and leaned over the bridges and kept thinking we were coming to a park when we weren't." And then the thing you almost apologise for — "it's a game I like to play — to walk and talk with the dead who smile and are silent, and free, quite finally free" — followed straight by "I suppose that sounds dreadful rubbish." It does not, and I wonder what it is about a companion who cannot answer that leaves the quais so open, when a real one would have made it an outing.

"I simply felt so happy that I leaned out of the window with my arms along the brass rail and my feet crossed and basked in the sunlight and the wonderful country unfolding."
