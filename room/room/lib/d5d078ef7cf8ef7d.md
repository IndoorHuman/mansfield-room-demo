"Walking up a dark hill with high iron fences at the sides of the road and immense trees over. I was looking for a midwife, Mrs. Nightingale." The road is dark and the errand is plain, and before anything else happens a hand arrives in yours — "A little girl, barefoot, with a handkerchief over her head pattered up and put her chill hand in mine; she would lead me."

She is the one who keeps score. "You should have believed me!" said the child, and dug her nails into my palm. And it is she who comes running at the end, "bursting with breathlessness dragging in her arms a huge black bag. "Mrs. Nightingale says you forgot this."" Then the whole thing turns over in one short sentence: "So / was the midwife." You do not stop to marvel at it. You walk on thinking: "I'll go and have a look at the poor little soul. But it won't be for a long time yet."

Everything in the first half is close and touchable — the jar of onions and a fork on the table, the square slippers seen through the fringe of the rug, the new-born baby that "fell forward on her lap" because the bundle was unwound too quickly. And there, in a room with an old hag and a fork, you write: "I felt the pleasure of the little girl beside me — a kind of quiver."

Then the page changes air entirely. "To sit in front of the little wood fire, your hands crossed in your lap and your eyes closed." The buttercup under the chin. "Still to taste the warm sunlight that melted in your mouth; still to smell the white waxy scent that lay upon the jonquil fields and the wild spicy scent of the rosemary growing in little tufts among the red rocks close to the brim of the sea...." The sea "dabbled with a pink the colour of unripe cherries," the sky with "a flying yellow light like the wings of canaries," the dogs who know the moon is there before anyone sees her.

I wonder whether the hill and the jonquil fields were set down in the same sitting, or whether the second came later and simply found the first waiting on the page.

At the foot of it stands one word, Death, with nothing after it. It is there and I am not going to talk over it. Above it, in your own hand, is this:

"I am tired, blissfully tired. Do you suppose that daisies feel blissfully tired when they shut for the night and the dews descend upon them?"
