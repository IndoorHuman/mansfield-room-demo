"Won a moral victory this morning, to my great relief. Went out to spend 2s. nd. and left it unspent. But I have never known a more hideous day. Terribly lonely." The victory and the hideous day are in the same breath, and neither one cancels the other — you set them down side by side and moved straight on to the trouble of writing itself: "Nothing that isn't satirical is really true for me to write just now. If I try to find things lovely, I turn pretty-pretty. And at the same time I am so frightened of writing mockery for satire that my pen hovers and won't settle."

And then, in the very same days, the pen does settle — twice, and both times on something seen rather than something judged. "No bird sits a tree more proudly than a pigeon. It looks at though placed there by the Lord. The sky was silky blue and white, and the sun shone through the little leaves." That is not pretty-pretty and it is not mockery. It is a pigeon being looked at properly.

The same on Swan Walk: "we came to Swan Walk and passed and repassed and remarked the delightful houses, white with flowering pear-trees in the gardens and green railings and fine carved gates. I want a little house very much." Passed and repassed — the wanting shows in the walking before it shows in the sentence.

You wrote, "My mind is full of embroidery, but there isn't any material to hold it together or make it strong." It is the pen hovering again, said another way. I notice the woman at the Cafe Royal gets held together perfectly well: "her arms crooked sharp at the elbows, her head thrust out, like a big bird." A big bird on one page and a proud pigeon on the next — did you notice you were seeing birds in everything that week, or did the birds simply arrive on their own?
