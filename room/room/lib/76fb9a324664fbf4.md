"Le travail, meme mauvais, vaut mieux que la reverie." It sits in a bracket at the top of the January page, in a second language, before anything else has begun — as if the sentence had to be said in French to be said at all.

Then the story starts and does not finish. A woman pinning on her hat, an unnamed thing "standing in the hall, doing nothing," a jacket snatched at mid-sentence. The page stops there and Marie comes in with the supper.

What follows is stern and unhurried: "But that is not important: what is, is that I have not written anything to-day worth a sou. I have passed the day in a kind of idleness. Why? Does it take so long to begin again?"

The questions keep coming and they are not rhetorical — they are asked one after another, each given its own mark, and no one answers them. "Is my desire to be idle greater than my desire to work?" Then the list, hard on itself: "My temper is bad; my personal habits are not above reproach; I'm ungracious — mentally untidy." Then the reform spreads outward until it reaches the letters themselves: "Even my handwriting. From this moment it too must change."

And the page closes on a condition rather than a promise: "But can I be honest? If I lie, it's no use."

What I keep turning over is that the entry opens with a maxim about bad work being better than dreaming and closes with a doubt about whether the writing can be truthful at all. Both are about the same page. I wonder which of the two you would say is the one that holds — the one that says do it badly, or the one that says only if it's true.
