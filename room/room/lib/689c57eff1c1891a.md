"Grandma's birthday. Where is that photograph of my dear love leaning against her husband's shoulder, with her hair parted so meekly and her eyes raised? I love it. I long to have it." The question is asked as though someone in the room might answer it, and then no one does, and the page goes on without the photograph.

Two reasons are given, and you rank them yourself. "For one thing Mother gave it me at a time when she loved me. But for another — so much more important — it is she, my own Grandma, young and lovely." The more important one is the one that is only looking: the arm, the baby sleeve, the velvet ribbon. "I must see them again."

And then the description that has not been written yet gets its own paragraph — "her beauty in her bath — when she was about sixty. Wiping herself with the towel." The fine linen, the throat, the scent. What I keep turning over is that you set the ribbon and the towel down side by side, one from a photograph you cannot find and one from memory you plainly can, and only the memory gets postponed.

"I have never really described her yet. Patience! The time will come."
