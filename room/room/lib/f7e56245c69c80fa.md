"1 got up to-day and felt better. It was intensely cold." Two facts side by side, and the second one does not undo the first.

Then M., and the hood — "a little blue hood fastened under the chin with a diamond clasp" — and you look at it long enough to say "She looked like a very ancient drawing." The clasp gets its own detail. The chin gets its own detail. And then in the same breath: "She suggested that if I did become cured, I might no longer write...." You let it stand with four dots after it and you do not argue with it, do not agree with it either. I notice the sentence about the hood is longer than the sentence about the cure.

The dream is where the writing opens all the way out. "Deep, almost violet blue waves with high foamy crests, and this white foam bore down on the blue in long curls." You call the seas "superb, unearthly (in the heavenly sense)" and stop to put the parenthesis in, so the word cannot be misread. Then: "The dream was about Chummie." The marriage without permission, the despair, and "I ' realised ' it was to be; what would have happened if he had not died." The quotation marks around realised are yours. Something in you would not let that word pass plain.

And the page ends: "Wingley made a dash at the bird window to-day."

I keep wondering about that order — the cold, the clasp, the sea, the brother, the cat — whether the last line arrived last because it happened last, or because it was the one that could be set down without anything after it.
