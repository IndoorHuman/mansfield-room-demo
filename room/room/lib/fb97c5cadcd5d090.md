"'The Aloe' is right. 'The Aloe' is lovely." Two sentences, one after the other, and the second one does not add anything the first did not already say — which is how you write when a thing has settled and you are only saying it again for the pleasure of saying it.

What comes before it is the correction: "when I had re-read it I knew that I was not quite 'right' yesterday." The manuscript arrives first, the verdict on yourself comes after. It is the finding that changes the day, not any decision you made about the day.

Then the plan, laid out fast and whole: "And now I know what the last chapter is." The birth, the coming in the autumn, the arms under the tree — "your solemnity, your wonderful beauty" — and "above all, your tremendous solemnity." You said solemnity twice too. 

"It simply fascinates me, and I know that it is what you would wish me to write." I notice the book is already promised out to people before it is finished: "All of them must have this book." And the next one is spoken of as though it were as certain as the first — "The next book will be yours and mine."

I keep wondering about "Oh, Bogey — I must hurry." Hurry is not usually the word of someone who has just found what they were looking for. Is it hurry toward the writing, or hurry because the shape came all at once and might go?

"It is good, my treasure!"
