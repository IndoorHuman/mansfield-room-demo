"In fact (perhaps you realise I am putting a terrific curb on myself) it was delicious. Only to breathe was enough." The curb is announced and then immediately not kept, because what follows is a train that waddled slowly round the lake, stopping at every tiny station, and a carriage full of huge Germans — "Vater und die Mama und Hanse" — who cried Schon! at every lilac-bush, and then read the notice about the cabinet aloud in the same order.

The facts keep arriving with the embroidery folded into them anyway. "Oh dear, you realize I'm just telling you facts. The embroidery I'll have to leave for now" — and the clock at Clarens has already turned out to be a cuckoo clock, and that has already been handed over as a question: "that seems to me awfully touching, doesn't it to you?"

The hotel gets a whole sentence to fail in: "Too clean. Spick is not the word, nor is span." Even the white lilac in the room is fresh from the laundry. And the pose, offered flatly, without apology — "a lady with a weak heart and lungs of Spanish leather. It seems to ' go down ' for the present."

What stays with me is that the curb and the delight are the same sentence. I keep wondering which one you thought you were putting down first.

"Only to breathe was enough."
