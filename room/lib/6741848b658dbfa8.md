"I have begun to sleep badly again and I've decided to tear up everything that I've written and start again. I'm sure that is best." The certainty in that second sentence sits oddly against the first, and you did not smooth it. Then: "This misery persists, and I am so crushed under it." No hedging there either. And the thing named as the trouble is not the misery itself but the shape of the work — "the slow building-up of my idea and then, before my eyes and out of my power, its slow dissolving."

Before one's eyes. The phrase comes back a day later doing something else entirely: "The trees are budding almost before one's eyes in this warm weather — big white buds like birds on" — and there the page breaks for A Moral Victory, and comes back with "the chestnut trees, and round trees just sprinkled with green." Same speed of watching, same helplessness in front of it, and this time it arrives as "The world is exceedingly lovely."

You keep counting things when you look outward. "Two had red sails and one had white." Not the boats generally — two and one.

What asks for something, in these, is other people. "Nothing helps or could help me except a person who could guess." Nobody guesses, and then it is "the noise of the water like rising waves" that gives what you called some vague comfort, and children, unnamed. And by the river, "I feel a real horror of people closing over me. I could not bear them. I wish I lived on a barge, with J. for a husband and a little boy for a son." The barge is the exception carved out of the horror — one husband, one son, one boat. Is it the smallness of that household you want, or the water under it?
