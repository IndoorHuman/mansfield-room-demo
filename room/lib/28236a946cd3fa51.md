"They are walking up and down the garden in Acacia Road. It is dusky; the Michaelmas daisies are bright as feathers." And then, before anything is explained, a pear falls — "a little round pear, hard as a stone" — and a hand picks it up and polishes it on a handkerchief, "unconsciously, as of old."

After that the page is almost nothing but two voices naming things. The pears were "so bright, canary yellow — and small." "The peel was so thin and the pips jet — jet black." You pulled out the little stem and sucked it, "and then you ate them always from the top — core and all." The ants got to them, and what is remembered of the ants is "that little round hole with a sort of fringe of brown pepper round it."

The seat gets the same treatment. "I shall never forget that pink garden seat. It is the only garden seat for me. Where is it now?" — and immediately after, without any answer to that, "It always wobbled a bit and there were usually the marks of a snail on it."

Midway there is one word sitting alone with no speaker attached to it, between "I feel it's as certain as this pear" and "I feel that too." It is not spoken by either of them. It is just there in the line, the way the moon is there over the pear tree and the ivy "glitter[s] like metal."

The piece does not soften that. It goes on to the cold, and the shivering, and the arm round her, and the good-byes, and it stops there.

What I keep turning over is the small unsolved thing they leave standing in the middle of all that inventory — "some of the pears we found used to have little teeth marks in them," and then, "Who bit them?" and then, "It was always a mystery." They had every other detail down to the pips. I wonder whether that one was kept unsolved on purpose, whether a garden needs one question left in it to stay a place you could still walk back into.

"I always see us walking about together, looking at things together with the same eyes, discussing."
