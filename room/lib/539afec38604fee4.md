"Heavily, more heavily than ever, falls the snow. It is hypnotising." And then, immediately, the looking that the sentence describes: "One looks, wonders vaguely how much has fallen and how much will fall and — looks again."

What follows that is a list with no seams in it. "Bandaged J.'s fingers. The Mercury came with At the Bay. I am very unsatisfied." Three things set down flat, side by side, the fingers and the magazine and the dissatisfaction all given the same amount of room.

Then cribbage, with nuts for counters. You go back a long way inside that sentence — "I used to play so often with such intense — Heavens with what feelings! — in the drawing-room at Carlton Hill while T played the piano" — and then you come straight back out of it: "But it meant absolutely nothing. J. giving me a bad nut and me paying him the bad nut again was all that really mattered." The bad nut passed back and forth is what you keep. I wonder whether that is a thing you decided on the page or something you already knew before you picked up the pen.

The day ends knitted and talked and read, and idle, and the reason given for the idleness is the weather: "We were idle — snow-bound."

"One feels there is nothing to be done while this goes on."
