"The women against the flowers were so lovely — even Miss S." That is the sentence in the middle of the page, and it is the one holding the most light in it, set down between a visit and a crying fit without anything to separate them.

The day opens shut: "No letters or papers." Then people arrive anyway — V., and Mrs. V., and Miss S. in white — and then someone's voice comes in whole and undiluted: "The trouble I've had with you, Mrs. Murry, and the expense it's put me to — more fuss than if you'd died there." It is given without answer. No reply is recorded, no defence, nothing softening it on either side. Just the words, kept.

And then the flowers, and the white, and the lovely.

And then: "I had a dreadful crying fit about ' noise and cleanliness.' It was horrible." The quotation marks around noise and cleanliness are doing something — as if the subject of the crying were being held slightly away, named in someone else's phrasing. It was horrible, and that is the last thing on the page. Nothing comes after to tidy it.

What stays with me is the order: the cruel sentence, then the loveliness, then the crying, all in sixty-odd words, none of them explained. I wonder whether the women against the flowers were beautiful because of the day or in spite of it — whether it was a relief to look up, or whether looking up is just what she did.
