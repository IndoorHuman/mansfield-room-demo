"Through the hall a man slipped quickly carrying what I thought was a cross of green leaves." You had already started the page elsewhere — 5.30, the clinique, the other man D., "I asked him to explain the treatment and so on. He did so." — and then you interrupted yourself: "But first:". The door opening as you approached it, the hall very light, "the maid smiling, wearing a little shawl, holding back the door." And then the green thing moves: "Suddenly the arms of the little cross waved feebly, and I saw it was a small child strapped to a wooden tray."

You put nothing after that. The next thing is the waiting, and the voices from another room, "M.'s over and above them: Da! Da!" and then, set apart, "and then an interrogatory: Da?"

The two feelings about M. arrive stacked, and you will not choose between them — the really good man and "a kind of unscrupulous impostor," with "sneaking" pulled out and defended in the same line. Then: "Another proof of my divided nature. All is disunited. Half boos, half cheers. Yes, that's it."

The hardest sentence is not decorated at all: "It's only by accident that I write anything worth a rush, and then it's only skimming the top — no more." And immediately beside it, in the same breath, the correction you make to yourself — "But remember The Daughters of the Late Colonel] was written at Mentone in November when I was not so bad as usual." That is twice on one page that you break your own account to put in something that would otherwise have gone unrecorded: once for the child on the tray, once for Mentone in November. I keep wondering whether those are the same motion in you — the "But first:" and the "But remember" — and what this page would look like if you had let it run straight through without either.

"I was trying with all my soul to be good."
