"Woke early and saw a snowy branch across the window." That is how the fourth opens, and then the weather gets its full account — "It is cold, snow has fallen, and now it is thawing. The hedges and the trees are covered with beads of water. Very dark, too, with a wind somewhere" — before the line that has nothing to do with weather at all: "I long to be alone for a bit." And straight after the wanting, with no bridge built between them: "I make a vow to finish a book this month. I'll write all day and at night too, and get it finished. I swear."

The vow is not new by the fourth. On the first it comes with a schedule: "First, get this book1 finished. That is a start. When? At the end of January." And then, "If I wrote night and day I could do it. Yes, I could. Right O!" On the second it has shrunk to a day: "I must finish my story tomorrow. I ought to work at it all day, — yes, all day and into the night if necessary." The same promise in three sizes, and each time the unit of measure is all day and into the night.

The second is a hard page and I am not going to make it lighter than you wrote it. "A horrible morning and afternoon. Je me sens incapable de tout, and at the same time I am just not writing very well." "Je me deteste aujourd'hui." "At times the fear of death is dreadful." It sits there in the middle of the week and the days on either side of it do not argue with it.

What the days on either side do have: on the first, "I went into the garden and opened the gate and nearly — just walked away," and "The shadow of the rose-tree lay on the grass like a tiny bouquet. The moon and the dew had put a spangle on everything." On the third, at the end of a day when "I could not get really warm," this: "If I lived alone I would be very dependent on poetry." That is a sentence about what you are made of, put down as though it were only a remark about the evening.

And the fifth. "Saw the sun rise. A lovely apricot sky with flames in it and then a solemn pink. Heavens, how beautiful!" Benny in the ivy, "He looked like an ivy bush himself," the fallen nests on the path, the early tea carried up. "I feel so full of love to-day after having seen the sun rise." And then, at the bottom, in two words and a half-sentence, the only day of the five where the work went: "Evening. Have written a good deal."

No vow on that page. No swearing, no all day and into the night. I keep turning that over — whether the writing came because the sun had been watched all the way up, or whether both of them simply belong to the same kind of morning, the kind you cannot promise yourself the night before.

"Evening. Have written a good deal."
