"I cough and cough, and at each breath a dragging, boiling, bubbling sound is heard. I feel that my whole chest is boiling. I sip water, spit, sip, spit. I feel I must break my heart." That is where the page begins, and it does not step back from itself afterwards: "And I can't expand my chest; it's as though the chest had collapsed...."

Then, in the same breath of writing, "Life is — getting a new breath. Nothing else counts."

What I keep turning over is what comes directly after. Not more of the chest, but a woman in a story: "Can't you help me? Can't you?" But even while she asked him she smiled as if it didn't matter so much whether he could or couldn't. And then a man with a friend, "small, shabby, with a wooden leg," and the tea asked for at the door — "Is that you, Beaty? Can we have some tea?" — and the line that breaks off three times before it ends: "Let the sparrow — let the sparrow — suffer the sparrow to...."

The work arrives on the same page as the boiling, with no seam between them. Roger commits suicide in charades, with a paper knife, and gurgles his life away — invented, staged, put down beside the real sipping and spitting.

I wonder about that dash in "Life is — getting a new breath." Whether it is punctuation or whether it is the breath itself, taken mid-sentence and written in.
