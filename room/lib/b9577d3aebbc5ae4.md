"It is all right. It is perfectly all right." K.T. says it into a road with a high hill on one side and a ravine on the other, and the cliffs at the base of it "shone like points of teeth, sharp and tiny." She says it again a paragraph later, and the second time the page calls it scolding — "It's quite all right. It's perfectly all right," scolded K.T. The same words, and by then they have turned into something a person does rather than something a person believes.

Then the horses gallop and do not arrive. "Though the horses maintained their tearing gallop, they were not coming towards her and her sister, but were galloping backwards." The muff goes over the sister's face. What finally passes is not the chariot at all but "a black horse as tall as a house with a dark serene rider in a wide hat gliding past them like a ship through the dark water."

And then, with no seam at all, the entry stands up and looks outside: "The view from my window this morning is so tremendously exciting. A high wind is blowing and the glass is dashed with rain." Pools of water in the timber-yard beside the cemetery, smoke blowing.

I keep thinking about that hinge — the burning dark of the bedroom on one line and the dashed glass on the next, and nothing set between them to explain the turn. Does the second half read to you as relief, or just as the next thing that happened to be there when the eyes opened?

A hand carrying something appears in the other page too. "One had a basket of chickweed on her arm, the other a basket of daffodils." Neat spare women with combed and braided hair, slinging talk across the bus, bread out of a sagging pocket, knees rocking, quick eyes going over the bus load.

"Busy and indifferent they looked."
