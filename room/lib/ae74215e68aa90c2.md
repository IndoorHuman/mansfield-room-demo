"The sea here is real sea. It rises and falls with a loud noise, has a long, silky roll on it as though it purred, seems sometimes to climb half up into the sky and you see the sail boats perched upon clouds — like flying cherubs."

The page begins there and does not stay there. Two lovers come along the front — "a pinched-in waist, a hat like a saucer turned upside down" — and what floats up is not romance but "Of course, occasional tinned meat does not matter, but a perpetual diet of tinned meat is bound to produce..." The sentence is left unfinished, and the blessing that follows it is given anyway: "I am sure that the Lord loves them and that they and their seed will prosper and multiply for ever and ever...."

And then the page turns into a list. Phrases with no home yet, some of them signed and some not: "The sense of folded flowers... as though the night had laid its hand upon their hearts and they were folded and at peace like folded flowers. (K.M.)" One is marked with a small verdict beside the initials — "The ostrich burying its head in the sand does at any rate wish to convey the impression that its head is the most important part of it. (K.M. Good.)"

What I keep going back to is the shortest one, which claims nothing and explains nothing:

"The high luxury of not having to explain...."

It sits in a list where every other line is doing work — the jewel "negotiable one day in the market of misery," the woman "so cold, so rich, so splendid," the one you could "go on cutting slice after slice" of and never find a plum or a cherry or even a piece of peel. Those are all reaching for someone. That one is not. I wonder, of the ones left unsigned, whether any of them were hers too and she simply did not bother to say so — whether the not having to explain was already being practised right there on the page.
