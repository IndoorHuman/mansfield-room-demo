"These last days I have been awfully rebellious. Longing for something. I feel uprooted. I want things that J. can so easily do without, that aren't natural to him. I long for them."

And then, a few lines on, without softening it: "Why am I troubled every single day of my life by the nearness of death and its inevitability? I am really diseased on that point. And I can't speak of it. If I tell J. it makes him unhappy. If I don't tell him, it leaves me to fight it. I am tired of the battle. No one knows how tired."

I am not going to move quickly past that. You wrote the evening-star and the mountains being lovely and sitting there thinking of death in the same breath, and you wrote "my body is a prison," and then "I am weak where I must be strong." Saturday gets one line — "less than ever. But no matter." A little progress is claimed: "I have realised what it is to be done — the strange barrier to be crossed from thinking it to writing it.... Daphne."

And the same page keeps going, straight into the First Lord, who is "worth attending to." "When you have spoken it, 'tis dead, and I am the grave of it." You liked that he was "Disillusioned and yet — amused — worldly, and yet he has feeling," and you saw him whole: "like a man on shipboard who is inclined to straddle just to show (but not to show off) how well his sea-legs serve him...." You copied out the line about "a mingled yarn, good and ill together" and said, of that temper, "I like the temper of that extremely."

Then the dozen soldiers "with delicate fine hats and most courteous feathers," which you called "a veritable little pageant," "Wonderfully sunny, with a little breeze." Then Parolles, "a lovable creature, a brave little cocksparrow of a ruffian." Then Helena, "a terrifying female," and the widow and D. and their cup of tea. Then the old King, "a queer old card."

I wonder whether the First Lord arrived the same evening as the evening-star, or whether they only ended up on the same page and you let them stay there.

On Hamlet you go further than notes: "So do we all begin by acting and the nearer we are to what we would be the more perfect our disguise." And then "Finally there comes the moment when we are no longer acting; it may even catch us by surprise. We may look in amazement at our no longer borrowed plumage.... acting has become action." And under all of it, four words: "The solitary person always acts."

Miranda gets the whole morning of the world — "that fair island still half dreaming in a golden haze — lapped about with little joyful hurrying waves of love." Juliet gets the dark and the chill dawn. You would not let anyone call them one.

"But I could write a thousand pages about Hamlets."
