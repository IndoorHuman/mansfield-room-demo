"1 begin to think of an unfinished memory which has been with me for years." That is the first line, and it is about a story called " Lena," and then within a paragraph the page has turned to someone else entirely: "Dear brother, as I jot these notes, I am speaking to you."

The question you ask yourself there is the one I keep rereading. "To whom did I always write when I kept those huge complaining diaries? Was it to myself?" You answer it immediately — "Yes, it is to you" — as though the answer had been waiting the whole time for the question to catch up.

What the entry does after that is not console. It states. "You are more vividly with me now this moment than if you were alive and I were writing to you from a short distance away." You do not soften that sentence at either end. It sits in the middle of the page with the fact of his death folded inside it and no cushioning at all.

And then, in the same breath as the great claim about not writing alone, the plainest admission on the page: "you know, with all my desire, my will is weak. To do things — even to write absolutely for myself and by myself — is awfully hard for me. God knows why, when my desire is so strong." Two things held at once, the doubt and the certainty, neither cancelling the other.

The part I would keep if I could only keep one is the list. "There are daisies on the table and a red flower, like a poppy, shines through. Of daisies I will write. Of the dark. Of the wind — and the sun and the mists. Of the shadows." It starts with what is actually on the table in front of you and then walks straight out of the room into weather.

I wonder whether the daisies were there before the entry began or whether you only saw them once you had decided what the book was going to be about.

"To-night it is made plain."
