" If I could only sweep all my garden up the hill, to your doors! " — and then, straight after, without a breath between them, "Her perfect little gesture as she said this." The whole garden offered, and what you kept was the hand that offered it.

Then the candlestick. "Many thanks for your stuffy letter." The letter is imaginary and the grudge is not: it was a gift, it came back, and it goes on going back — "Or shall I first send it to you as an early Christmas present and do you return it as a late one or a New Year's gift." Easter gets ruled out on grounds of taste. "It would be a trifle excessive at Easter." The joke is built like a small machine, every turn of it tighter than the last, until it runs past both of you: "I wonder which of us will be in possession of it at the last." A will, a burial, and then the thing escapes altogether — "a fliegende candlestick, in fact!"

And below all that invention, four flat words about the body: "I have been suffering from wind round the heart." You give it no more room than that, then take the tone back at once — "Such a tiresome complaint, but not dangerous." What you object to is not the pain but the arrangement: "Really, for anything to be so painful I think I would prefer a spice of danger added."

The page holds a gesture, a candlestick that will not stay given, and a complaint you would like better if it were grander. I keep wondering whether the last line was written as a report or as a small piece of triumph.

"The first act was brought on by a fit of laughing."
