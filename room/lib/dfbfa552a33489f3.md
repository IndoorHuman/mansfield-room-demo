"H. is a man to remember. At tea that day." That is the whole opening, two short sentences, and then you go straight to the table — the huge silver kettle, the pots, the large plates, and the cake you tell yourself twice about: "The ornate cake; one must remember that cake."

What gets remembered around it is small and exact. The old hand grasping the knife calmly. The fat finger on the knob while the tea goes from kettle to cup. The lace scarf gathered into a lap. You let the talk cross itself without smoothing it — the answer that is "No, thanks. Yes. Half a cup."

Then the page turns from the tea things to the man, and stays with him. "H. always collects something — always will." China, silver, fiddles, "any old thing that comes along." "His feeling for his children is so tender that it's pain. He can't understand it." And beside that, the thing you say must also be remembered: "his extraordinary insecurity. The world rocks under him, and it's only when he has that stethoscope that he can lay down the law."

You hear what he does not say out loud. "you hear the unspoken: ' I am a plain man, you know....'"

The last paragraph does not soften. "He feels somewhere that all is ashes." Church puts his heart at rest, and then beef and restlessness. Flies in childhood. "And I still see suicide as his end, in a kind of melancholia, and ' nobody wants me,' and ' damned if I won't.'" You write it as a foresight, in his own borrowed phrases, and you stop there — no line after it to take the weight off.

I keep wondering about the order of it: the cake first, insisted on, and the whole of him after. Did the cake come to you first when you sat down to write, or did you put it there because you knew what the page was going to have to hold?
