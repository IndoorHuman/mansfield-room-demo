"Bateson and his love of the louse for its own sake. Pedigree lice." That is where the page starts, and it stays there a while — the boxes, the glands, the lice feeding on his arm, the cases reduced from 7000 to 700, "No reward, not even an O.B.E." You copied down the whole apparatus of it before you said a word about yourself.

Then the parasites, one after another, the hydatids and the water-snail and the rabbits, and out of all that you wrote: "It made me feel how perfect the world is, with its worms and hooks and ova, how incredibly perfect. There is the sky and the sea and the shape of a lily, and there is all this other as well. The balance how perfect! (Salut, Tchehov!) I would not have the one without the other."

The sentence just before it you crossed out in your own way — "No, that's not what I mean" — and then said it again, better. I notice you did not do that anywhere else on the page.

And immediately after: "The clocks are striking ten. Here in my room the sky looks lilac; in the bath-room it is like the skin of a peach. Girls are laughing."

Then: "I have consumption. There is still a great deal of moisture (and pain) in my bad lung. But I do not care. I do not want anything I could not have. Peace, solitude, time to write my books, beautiful external life to watch and ponder — no more. O, I'd like a child as well — a baby boy; mats je demande trop!"

That sits where it sits. You put the list of what you want in the same breath as the lung, and the list is not small — books, solitude, the watching and pondering — and then the one thing you called too much to ask for, and the French to say it in.

The rest of the page goes off into other people. The mosquito "a lovely little high stepping gazelle" and the wasp "a fierce roaring tiger" at the honey dish, and you told them both: "Drink, my darlings!" L.M. and the cold coffee, looking "mysterious and important, as if, as a matter of fact she had known all along that this was a cold coffee day." Miss Todd and Miss Hopper second violins, Miss Bray a viola. The bells at midday, "some velvety soft, some languid, some regretful, and one impatient — a youthful bell ringing high and quick above the rest," and someone in there thinking joyfully, "That's the bell for me!"

I keep wondering whether you heard those bells that day or whether they arrived already sorted into their tempers, the impatient one picked out before the pen touched the page.
