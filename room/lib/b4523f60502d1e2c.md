"Paris? To remember the sound of wind — the peculiar wretchedness one can feel while the wind blows." That is where it starts, and then the wind keeps coming: "the warm soft wind of spring searching out the heart," "the wind I call the Ancient of Days which blows here at night," "the wind that shakes the garden at night when one runs out into it," "the wind of summer, so playful, that rocked and swung in the trees here."

Eight or nine winds, each one given its own clause and no more, until one of them will not stay a clause: "And wind moving through grass so that the grass quivers. This moves me with an emotion I don't ever understand."

And then, straight after the not-understanding, the page turns specific in a way none of the winds were. "I always see a field, a young horse — and there is a very fair Danish girl telling me something about her stepfather. The girl's name is Elsa Bagge."

The name is the last thing you needed from that. Not what she said about the stepfather, not the field, not the horse — the name, written out in full, as though the whole list of winds had been the way round to reaching it. I keep wondering whether she arrived with the grass every time, or whether the grass has been arriving with her.
