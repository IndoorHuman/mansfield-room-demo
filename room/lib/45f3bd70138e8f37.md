"I have been thinking this morning until it seems I may get things straightened out if I try to write... where I am." The straightening is the writing; the sentence says as much before it does anything else, and then the page goes on to be as unsparing as it promised. "Ever since I came to Paris I have been as ill as ever." "I can only walk to the taxi and back. I get up at midi and go to bed at 5.30." "My spirit is nearly dead." Nothing there is arranged to be easier than it was.

And then, midway, without ceremony: "Ah, I feel a little calmer already to be writing." "Thank God for writing!"

What holds the second half of the page together is that she turns and asks herself a question by name. "Now, Katherine, what do you mean by health? And what do you want it for?" The answer is longer than the question and it faces entirely outward — "the earth and the wonders thereof — the sea — the sun" — and it gets to a place where the sentence has to admit its own delay: "(and here I have stopped and waited and waited and it's no good — there's only one phrase that will do) a child of the sun."

The wanting is specific after that. "I want a garden, a small house, grass, animals, books, pictures, music." "I want so to live that I work with my hands and my feeling and my brain." And the aside that will not be talked out of itself: "(Though I may write about cabmen. That's no matter.)"

The page does not stop with her. Another hand takes it in brackets, and after that hand there is a note about the type, Caslon's letters, "noted for their extreme legibility." That is simply how the book ends where it ends.

What I keep turning over is the pause inside the parenthesis — the stopping and waiting that got written down instead of being tidied away. Does a phrase like that one arrive because of the waiting, or only once the waiting is given up on?

"I feel happy — deep down. All is well."
