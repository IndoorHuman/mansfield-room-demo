"I am sad to-night. Perhaps it is the old forlorn wind." Two sentences, and then the reason moves — "the thought of you spiritually is not enough to-night. I want you by me" — and then the reason moves again, into work: "I must get deep down into my book, for then I shall be happy. Lose myself, lose myself to find you, dearest." Three different places to put the same sadness, none of them cancelling the one before it.

What follows the sadness is a door handle. "I remember standing on tiptoe and using both hands to turn the big white china door-handle." The whole recollection is built out of things at a child's height: a hand "scarcely bigger than my doll's, in a frilled sleeve," the brass knobs of the big bed that the sun "winked on," "the silver brooch that was a half-moon with five little owls sitting on it."

And the doll's house, which arrives before the sister does. "It was a beautiful one with a verandah and a balcony and a door that opened and shut and two chimneys. I wanted badly to show it to someone else." Twice the child asks when Gwen will play with it, and twice she is told by-and-by.

The hard line is put down flatly, in someone else's mouth, in the kitchen: "Kep' alive on bullock's blood hotted in a saucer over a candle." And directly after it — "After that I felt frightened of Gwen" — comes the bargain about the stairs, that she would not let her go upstairs into the bedroom, "only downstairs, and then only when I saw she could look." There is no explanation offered for the fright and none for the bargain. The petals fall out of the shawl, "little white petals like feathers," and "Gwen did not look, even then." Then a man with his head in a black bag, and "I saw my grandmother's face change as she bent over little Gwen," and then: "The picture was hung over the nursery fire. I thought it looked very nice. The doll's house was in it — verandah and balcony and all."

The page turns over to college after that with no seam at all. "I was thinking yesterday of my wasted, wasted early girlhood" — and what follows is not waste as anyone else would draw it: "I lived in the girls, the professor, the big, lovely building, the leaping fires in winter and the abundant flowers in summer. The views out of the windows, all the pattern that was — weaving. Nobody saw it, I felt, as I did." Then: "My mind was just like a squirrel. I gathered and gathered and hid away, for that long 'winter' when I should rediscover all this treasure — and if anybody came close I scuttled up the tallest, darkest tree and hid in the branches."

I keep setting that beside the child who had "gone all through it myself, from the kitchen to the dining-room, up into the bedrooms with the doll's lamp on the table, heaps and heaps of times" — going through a thing over and over on her own, and wanting badly to show it to someone. Are those two the same wanting, do you think, the squirrel and the girl on the stairs of the doll's house?

And Cramb, who gets the plainest joy on the page: "Ageless and fiery, eating himself up again and again, very fierce at what he had seen, but going a bit blind because he had looked so long." "Cramb striding up and down, filled me up to the brim. I couldn't write down Cramb's thunder. I simply wanted to sit and hear him."

"I wanted badly to show it to someone else."
