"To-day, this evening, after I have come home (for I must go out and buy some fruits) commence encore une vie nouvelle. Turn over and you'll see how good I become — a different child." And then, a line down, only "Later."

The later does not report back on the different child. It goes off to reading instead, and comes back with "I must start writing again," and after that with the question you set out under its own heading and answered by circling it: "What is it that makes the moment of delivery so difficult for me?" The stories are already there — "they are so word perfect" — and still: "I haven't a place to write in or on — the chair isn't comfortable — yet even as I complain this seems the place and this the chair." The complaint and the staying put arrive in one breath, joined by a dash, neither one winning.

Then the small confessions that ask for nothing: "I must not forget my timidity before closed doors. My debate as to whether I shall ring too loud or not loud enough."

And then the street takes over and the difficulty is simply not mentioned again. "The tide is full in the Redcliffe Road." "On these summer evenings the sound of the steps along the street is quite different. They knock-knockknock along, but lightly and easily, as though they belonged to people who were walking home at their ease, after a procession or a pic-nic or a day at the sea." The primrose, the poor dog, Miss Ruddick and her resin gummed on a flannel — all of it seen from what you call that remote bedroom, where "all that happens beyond is marvellous."

I keep thinking about the piano figure — "until suddenly, you realize you are warm — you have only just begun to play" — and wondering whether you had already gone out for the fruits by the time the page filled up, or whether they waited.

"On the wall of the kitchen there was a shadow, shaped like a little mask with two gold slits for eyes. It danced up and down."
