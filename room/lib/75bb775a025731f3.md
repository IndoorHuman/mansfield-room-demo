"It was rather a horrible day. I was ill, and at night I had one of my terrible fits of temper over a parcel. Is it possible one can be so unruly?" The parcel does not get described. What it held, who sent it, whether it was opened — none of that is on the page. The question is what you keep instead, and you leave it standing without an answer.

Then, a few lines on, the pain gets an instruction attached to it. "Put it on record, in case it ever goes, what a pain it is. Remember to give it to someone in a story one day." That is the working mind arriving in the middle of a bad night — not to make the sciatica smaller, but to file it where it can be used. The record is kept in case it ever goes. You wrote it down against the possibility of forgetting a thing you would surely rather forget.

And the last of it is all looking. "Remember her eyes — the pupils dark — black — and her whiteness. Even her hair seems to grow pale. She folded the quilt and held it in her arms as though it was a baby." No comment after the quilt. The seeing goes on working on a day you called horrible, and the page ends on someone else's arms.

The morning's news sits at the top, quite flat: "Massingham accepts the idea of a regular story." Accepted, and then the day happened over it. I keep wondering whether the acceptance and the temper knew about each other at all.
