"I finished Mr. and Mrs. Dove yesterday. I am. not altogether pleased with it. It's a little bit made up. It's not inevitable." The complaint is not that it is bad. It is that it could have gone otherwise, and a thing that could have gone otherwise is not yet the thing.

Then the trial runs both ways at once. "I mean to imply that those two may not be happy together — that that is the kind of reason for which a young girl marries. But have I done so? I don't think so." You put the intention down and refuse it credit in the same breath.

"I want to be nearer — far, far nearer than that. I want to use all my force even when I am taking a fine line." A fine line and all the force — those do not sound like they belong together, and you write them as though they obviously do.

The sharpest part is where you catch yourself using your own people: "I have, at the end, used the Doves unwarrantably." And then the question you ask and answer without pausing — "I used them to round off something — didn't I? Is that quite my game? No, it's not. It's not quite the kind of truth I'm after." I notice you do not say what kind of truth it is. Only what it is not. Is the naming of it something that comes later, or is not-that as close as it ever gets said?

"Now for Susannah. All must be deeply felt."

And then the turn, at the very end, where the difficulty stops being about the Doves at all: "But what is one to do, with this wretched cat and mouse act? There's my difficulty! I must try to write this afternoon instead. There is no reason why I shouldn't! No reason, except the after-effects of pain on a weakened organism."

The exception arrives last, after the exclamation, in the flattest words on the page.
