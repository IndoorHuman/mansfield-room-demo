"I thought and thought this morning but to not much avail." That is the first line, and then almost at once the correction: "I am all right — sky-high." Not tired, not low — the trouble is the getting down. "in my brain, in my head, I can think and act and write wonders — wonders; but the moment I really try to put them down I fail miserably."

And yet the page keeps going, and what it fills with is a list of everything waiting. Geneva is long, Hamilton is very short, one for the brother, one about the life in New Zealand, then Bavaria, then Paris. "God! When shall I write all these things and how?" The wit that was deserting is right there naming six things at once.

Then the small voices come through without permission — "I'd like to have a kitten about as big as a little tea-pot" — and the man who beats you, the quiet one absorbed in his work, and the verdict on him: "It is all a bit dimcult to write, but awfully fascinating, and should not be at too great length."

What stays with me is the interruption near the bottom, the one that has nothing to do with any of it: "Does this pen write? Oh, I do hope so. For it's really beastly to have a pen that doesn't." A whole morning of not being able to put things down, and the last worry is the instrument.

Did the pen turn out to be a good one?
