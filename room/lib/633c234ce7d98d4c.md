"Feature Extraordinary: Shoes that have never squeaked before start up a squeaking." It is the first line of June and it is set out like news, capitals and all, as though the shoes were the event of the month. Then the weather comes in underneath it: "A cold day — the cuckoo singing and the sea like liquid metal."

After that the whole page is about things coming loose. "Everything feels detached — uprooted — flying through the hurtling air or about to fly." Then the birds, which you call "unnatural rudderless birds," and then the picture you say is homely: "imagine the world an immense drying ground with everything blown off the lines." And having drawn it, you weigh it plainly — "It is very nervously exhausting." The squeaking shoes belong to that same untethered world, I think, only they got the headline and the drying ground got the explanation.

Mrs. Honey's name sits on its own line before anything about her is said. When she does arrive she has been crying: fifteen rooms, three nights of stairs at sixty-eight, spoken to "awful crool" about a cracked tumbler, and "couldn't help but cry...." What follows is not softened — the wish that Madame "develop a tumour during the night," the line "exactly like a large cow in a black silk dress — and she will never, never, NEVER die." You let the rage stand at full length and then set beside it, with nothing between, Mrs. Honey's own saying: "If the fire turns bright, your maan is in a good temper." Her sentence gets the last word of that stretch, and it is the only cheerful thing on the page.

Then the pink bed cover turned down to check the blankets, and the vision of doing the same for a son in years to come — and immediately: "The moment had no emotional value at all — especially as it was all drowned in the smell of roast mutting." You do that more than once here. You let something large arrive and then name what was actually in the room instead: mutton, a gong that "sounds like a timid fire alarm," a table laid for two and "a white serviette — shaped like a hand with spread fingers."

And then the genet fleuri rubbed on the collar, and the sentence the whole day seems to have been walking toward, which is about being an instrument and not a thing put away:

"I look different — as though I were meant to be played on and not just to lie in a corner."
