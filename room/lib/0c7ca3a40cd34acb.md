"Finished An Ideal Family yesterday. It seems to me better than The Doves, but still it's not good enough." You get the comparison and the verdict into one line, and then you spend the rest of the entry refusing to be comforted by the first half of it. "I worked at it hard enough, God knows, and yet I didn't get the deepest truth out of the idea, even once." The measure is not effort. You say so plainly.

Then the sharpest thing in the page: "This looks and smells like a story, but I wouldn't buy it. I don't want to possess it — to live with it. NO." You judge it as a person in a shop would, by smell, by whether you would take it home. And the NO is its own sentence in capitals, which is not something you do often.

What follows is a plan with a number in it. "Once I have written two more, I shall tackle something different — a long story: At the Bay, with more difficult relationships. That's the whole problem." Two more first. The difficulty is named as the point, not the obstacle.

And then the page turns into things seen. The bag opened and peered into and shook. "And a very long shining blue-black hairpin gleaming on the faded carpet...." Mr. Bailey in his blue apron, one hand on his hip, the other on the handle of a long knife stuck into a huge chopping block. The white flower in the buttonhole "as though it were made of a curl of muttonfat." Nothing in the entry explains why these are set down here, next to the verdict that the finished thing was not good enough. They arrive with no defence.

The July 18 page keeps the same eye and turns it on the house. "The noise in this house this morning is sheer hell." The flowers to do, the laundry, the Palace at eleven. "But I bitterly long for a little private room where I can work undisturbed. The balcony is not good enough; neither is this salon." Two rooms tried and rejected in one clause — the same shopkeeper's test as the story that looked and smelled right and would not be bought.

And then, on a page written near all that, there is a doorway with a lamp in it and jasmine overhead, and someone chanting to the person arriving cold:

"Be not afraid, the house is full of blankets, Red ones and white ones, lovely beyond dreaming, Key-pattern, tasselled, camel-hair and woolly, Softer than sleep or the bosom of a swan."

I keep wondering about the order of these — whether the blankets were already in your head while the maid plodded up with the breakfast things, or whether the welcome only became sayable once the noise had a room of its own on the page. The entry does not say, and the note after it says only where it was found: "[In the middle of the manuscript of Her First Ball.]"
